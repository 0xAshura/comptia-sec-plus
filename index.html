First understand the mental model

Security+ Domain 2 becomes easy when you connect every question to the same chain: who attacks, how they enter, what weakness they abuse, what evidence appears, and what control reduces the risk.

1. ActorWho is behind it? Nation-state, criminal group, insider, hacktivist, unskilled attacker, or shadow IT.
2. MotivationWhy? Money, espionage, revenge, political belief, blackmail, disruption, war, or convenience.
3. VectorHow do they reach the target? Email, SMS, vulnerable app, open port, vendor, Wi-Fi, USB, voice call.
4. VulnerabilityWhat weakness makes it work? Misconfiguration, SQLi, XSS, zero-day, default password, legacy system.
5. Indicator + mitigationWhat shows up and what control helps? Logs, lockouts, resource spikes, segmentation, MFA, patching, monitoring.
Exam mindset: read scenario words carefully. “One password attempted across many accounts” = password spraying. “Many passwords against one account” = brute force. “Victim visits a compromised site they normally trust” = watering hole. “Same user logs in from impossible geography” = indicator, not an attack type.

2.1 — Threat actors and motivations

A threat actor is the person, group, or process creating risk. The exam wants you to compare actors by internal/external position, resources, sophistication, and motivation.

Nation-state / APT

externalhigh fundingpatient

Meaning: A government-backed or government-aligned actor with time, tooling, intelligence access, and strategic goals.

Motivation: Espionage, war preparation, influence operations, sabotage, intellectual-property theft.

Real-life mental picture: A supply-chain compromise such as SolarWinds shows why trusted software updates can become a strategic intrusion path.

Common signals: Long dwell time, stealthy persistence, living-off-the-land commands, identity abuse, careful exfiltration.

Defensive focus: Threat intel, EDR/XDR, privileged access management, segmentation, verified software supply chain, detection engineering mapped to ATT&CK.

Organized crime

externalfinancialscalable

Meaning: Professionally run criminal groups, often using ransomware-as-a-service, botnets, credential marketplaces, and affiliate models.

Motivation: Financial gain, blackmail, extortion, resale of access, fraud.

Real-life mental picture: Ransomware incidents such as Colonial Pipeline illustrate how credential abuse and extortion can disrupt real business operations.

Common signals: Phishing, stolen credentials, mass scanning, data staging, ransom notes, unusual encryption activity.

Defensive focus: MFA, tested backups, EDR, email security, egress monitoring, rapid isolation, least privilege, vulnerability prioritization with KEV.

Hacktivist

externalideologicalloud

Meaning: Actor motivated by political, social, or ideological causes rather than direct profit.

Motivation: Public embarrassment, service disruption, data leaks, attention, protest.

Real-life mental picture: DDoS, website defacement, doxing, and leaked internal chat screenshots are common hacktivist-style outcomes.

Common signals: Public claims of responsibility, coordinated traffic spikes, politically timed targeting, social media amplification.

Defensive focus: DDoS protection, incident communications plan, attack-surface reduction, web hardening, monitoring of brand abuse.

Insider threat

internaltrusted accesshard to notice

Meaning: A current or former employee, contractor, vendor, or partner who misuses legitimate access accidentally or intentionally.

Motivation: Revenge, financial gain, coercion, negligence, convenience, policy bypass.

Real-life mental picture: A departing employee downloading client lists or a contractor overusing admin rights both count as insider risk.

Common signals: Large exports, off-hours access, access to unrelated projects, unusual USB use, sudden permission changes.

Defensive focus: Least privilege, separation of duties, user behavior analytics, DLP, logging, fast deprovisioning, manager review of access.

Unskilled attacker / script kiddie

externallow capabilityopportunistic

Meaning: An attacker who relies on public tools, leaked credentials, automated scanners, and copied tutorials.

Motivation: Curiosity, bragging rights, chaos, low-effort profit, revenge.

Real-life mental picture: Publicly exposed admin panels with default passwords are common targets because they require little skill.

Common signals: Noisy scans, repeated failed logins, obvious tool user-agents, attacks against old CVEs.

Defensive focus: Patch quickly, remove default credentials, close unused ports, rate-limit auth, monitor internet-facing assets.

Shadow IT

internalunapproved techoften accidental

Meaning: Technology used without formal IT/security approval, such as personal cloud storage, unsanctioned SaaS, unmanaged scripts, or side databases.

Motivation: Convenience, speed, business pressure, lack of awareness; not always malicious.

Real-life mental picture: A team exporting customer data into a personal spreadsheet app creates exposure even if nobody intended harm.

Common signals: Unknown OAuth apps, unsanctioned SaaS traffic, data in unmanaged repositories, duplicate asset inventories.

Defensive focus: SaaS discovery, CASB/SSE, policy, approved alternatives, procurement review, data classification, user education.

Quick memory: Nation-state = patience and espionage. Organized crime = money and extortion. Hacktivist = ideology and attention. Insider = trusted access. Unskilled attacker = public tools. Shadow IT = unapproved technology, often not malicious.

2.2 — Threat vectors and attack surfaces

A threat vector is the path used to attack. An attack surface is the total set of exposed paths. Reducing attack surface means giving attackers fewer ways to interact with your environment.

Message-based threats: email, SMS, IM

vectorattack surface

Core idea: Attackers use messages because identity trust is easier to abuse than technical controls. Phishing emails, smishing texts, and chat-based pretexts try to make the victim click, approve, install, pay, or disclose. The exam angle is to notice the medium and the requested action.

Simple scenario: Employee receives a fake Microsoft 365 alert, clicks a lookalike login page, and the attacker steals a session token.

Controls that make sense: Email authentication, secure gateways, phishing-resistant MFA, user reporting button, URL rewriting, attachment sandboxing, conditional access.

Image-based and file-based vectors

vectorattack surface

Core idea: Images and files can carry malicious macros, exploit vulnerable previewers, hide QR links, or trick users with double extensions. The file itself may not be the final payload; it may be a delivery mechanism.

Simple scenario: Invoice.pdf.html opens in a browser and asks for credentials; a QR code in a poster sends users to a fake benefits portal.

Controls that make sense: Block risky attachments, disable macros from the internet, content disarm/reconstruction, file reputation, EDR, training on QR phishing.

Voice call / vishing

vectorattack surface

Core idea: The attacker uses a phone call to bypass written evidence and create urgency. Modern vishing often targets help desks, MFA reset processes, and executives.

Simple scenario: Caller pretends to be an employee locked out before a deadline and convinces support to reset MFA.

Controls that make sense: Help-desk identity proofing, callbacks to known numbers, anti-SIM-swap procedures, ticket audit logs, training on urgency scripts.

Removable device

vectorattack surface

Core idea: USB drives can be used for malware delivery, data theft, or human curiosity attacks. The real issue is untrusted hardware gaining direct endpoint access.

Simple scenario: A branded USB left in a parking lot is plugged into a workstation and executes a malicious shortcut.

Controls that make sense: Disable autorun, block unknown USB storage, device control, user training, EDR, physical security.

Vulnerable software

vectorattack surface

Core idea: Software with known or unknown flaws becomes an entry point. Internet-facing apps, VPNs, edge devices, and exposed management interfaces are especially dangerous.

Simple scenario: Attackers exploit an unpatched file-transfer application and steal data before the organization sees a normal patch cycle.

Controls that make sense: Asset inventory, vulnerability scanning, KEV-driven prioritization, patch SLAs, WAF/IPS as compensating control, external attack-surface monitoring.

Unsupported systems and legacy apps

vectorattack surface

Core idea: Unsupported systems no longer receive normal security updates. Legacy apps may require old libraries, weak ciphers, or unsupported OS versions.

Simple scenario: A production Windows server remains unpatched because a business app breaks on newer versions.

Controls that make sense: Isolate, virtual patch, restrict access, plan replacement, compensating controls, remove from internet, document accepted risk.

Unsecure networks: wireless, wired, Bluetooth

vectorattack surface

Core idea: A network becomes a threat vector when attackers can intercept, impersonate, jam, or join it without adequate controls.

Simple scenario: Guest Wi-Fi shares a flat subnet with internal printers and admin interfaces.

Controls that make sense: Network segmentation, WPA3/802.1X, NAC, VPN on untrusted networks, disable unused Bluetooth, rogue AP detection.

Open service ports

vectorattack surface

Core idea: Every listening service is an attack surface. The issue is not only the port number, but the exposed service, version, authentication, and reachability.

Simple scenario: A forgotten SSH service on a public VM allows password spraying against a local admin account.

Controls that make sense: Close unused services, firewall allow lists, bastion/jump hosts, strong auth, service inventory, exposure scanning.

Default credentials

vectorattack surface

Core idea: Default passwords convert deployment mistakes into immediate compromise, especially for IoT, routers, cameras, apps, and admin portals.

Simple scenario: Botnet scans find cameras using admin/admin and enroll them into DDoS traffic.

Controls that make sense: Change defaults during build, enforce unique passwords, vendor hardening checklist, block public admin panels.

Supply chain: MSPs, vendors, suppliers

vectorattack surface

Core idea: Instead of attacking the final victim directly, attackers compromise software updates, vendors, service providers, dependencies, or hardware sources.

Simple scenario: A trusted vendor update contains malicious code, so customers install the intrusion path themselves.

Controls that make sense: Vendor risk management, SBOM, code signing, update validation, least-privileged vendor access, continuous monitoring, contractual security requirements.

Phishing, smishing, pretexting, BEC

vectorattack surface

Core idea: These are human-vector attacks. The attacker creates believable context, then asks for credentials, money, approval, or sensitive data.

Simple scenario: Fake CFO email asks finance to change vendor banking information minutes before payroll cutoff.

Controls that make sense: MFA, payment verification, anti-spoofing controls, domain monitoring, security awareness, separation of duties.

Watering hole and brand impersonation

vectorattack surface

Core idea: The attacker compromises or imitates a site the victim group naturally visits. The victim comes to the attacker instead of receiving a direct lure.

Simple scenario: A fake careers page for a known company collects resumes and login credentials from targeted applicants.

Controls that make sense: Web filtering, browser isolation, certificate monitoring, brand takedowns, threat intel, patch browsers quickly.

Misinformation / disinformation / typosquatting

vectorattack surface

Core idea: The goal is to manipulate trust. Typosquatting uses lookalike domains; disinformation uses false narratives to influence behavior.

Simple scenario: paypaI.example uses a capital I instead of l, while a social media post claims a fake emergency login page is official.

Controls that make sense: Domain monitoring, DMARC/DKIM/SPF, user reporting, brand protection, trusted communication channels.

Social engineering pattern: attackers use authority, urgency, scarcity, fear, helpfulness, or familiarity. The mitigation is not only “train users.” Strong processes, MFA, verification workflows, and technical controls must support users.

2.3 — Types of vulnerabilities

A vulnerability is a weakness. It can be a code flaw, design flaw, configuration mistake, outdated system, weak process, or misplaced trust relationship.

Memory injection

vulnerability

What it is: Code or data is inserted into a legitimate process so malicious behavior appears to come from a trusted process.

Example: Credential theft malware injects into a browser process to read session data.

Indicators: Unexpected child processes, unsigned modules in trusted processes, suspicious memory regions, EDR alerts.

Mitigation: Exploit protection, EDR, application control, least privilege, patching, restrict scripting tools.

Buffer overflow

vulnerability

What it is: A program writes more data than a memory buffer can safely hold, causing crash, corruption, or possible code execution.

Example: A network service fails to validate input length and crashes when sent oversized input.

Indicators: Crashes, segmentation faults, exploit protection alerts, unusual service restarts.

Mitigation: Memory-safe languages, bounds checking, ASLR/DEP, fuzz testing, patching, input validation.

Race condition / TOC-TOU

vulnerability

What it is: Security check and security use happen at different times, allowing the state to change in between.

Example: App checks a file permission, then attacker swaps the file before the app writes to it.

Indicators: Inconsistent logs, rare privilege errors, file changes milliseconds apart, hard-to-reproduce bugs.

Mitigation: Atomic operations, file locks, secure temp-file handling, transaction controls, code review.

Malicious update

vulnerability

What it is: A trusted update channel is abused to deliver malicious code or backdoored software.

Example: Supply-chain actor inserts a backdoor into a signed software build.

Indicators: Update from valid vendor but unusual network connections after install, new services, unexpected binaries.

Mitigation: Code signing, reproducible builds, vendor monitoring, staged rollout, EDR, update integrity checks, SBOM.

Operating system vulnerabilities

vulnerability

What it is: Weaknesses in OS kernels, services, drivers, or permission models can allow privilege escalation, remote compromise, or sandbox escape.

Example: Local attacker exploits an unpatched kernel flaw to become admin/root.

Indicators: Privilege escalation, new admin accounts, kernel crash logs, suspicious driver loading.

Mitigation: Patch OS, remove unsupported versions, secure baseline, endpoint protection, least privilege.

SQL injection

vulnerability

What it is: Untrusted input changes a database query because the app mixes code and data.

Example: Login form treats input as SQL logic and returns data without proper authorization.

Indicators: Database errors in web logs, abnormal query volume, dumped tables, WAF alerts.

Mitigation: Parameterized queries/prepared statements, ORM safely used, input validation, least-privileged DB users, WAF.

Cross-site scripting (XSS)

vulnerability

What it is: Untrusted input is rendered as executable script in another user's browser.

Example: A comment field stores script that steals session data from every admin viewing the page.

Indicators: Suspicious script in user content, CSP violations, user reports of redirects/popups.

Mitigation: Output encoding, content security policy, input validation, framework auto-escaping, secure cookies.

Hardware vulnerabilities: firmware, EOL, legacy

vulnerability

What it is: Flaws in firmware, embedded controllers, or aging hardware can persist below the OS and may be hard to inspect.

Example: A router with outdated firmware exposes a remote admin vulnerability.

Indicators: Unknown firmware versions, vendor EOL notices, unusual device traffic, no patch path.

Mitigation: Firmware inventory, vendor lifecycle tracking, secure boot, signed firmware, replacement planning.

Virtualization vulnerabilities: VM escape, resource reuse

vulnerability

What it is: A guest system may break isolation or recover data left behind from another tenant or VM.

Example: Compromised VM attacks hypervisor interface or reads uncleaned virtual disk blocks.

Indicators: Hypervisor alerts, abnormal inter-VM traffic, unauthorized snapshots, resource spikes.

Mitigation: Patch hypervisor, isolate tenants, wipe resources before reuse, restrict host tools, separate management network.

Cloud-specific vulnerabilities

vulnerability

What it is: Cloud risk often comes from identity, exposed storage, permissive security groups, metadata service abuse, and misconfigured managed services.

Example: Public object storage bucket exposes customer backups because a policy allows anonymous read.

Indicators: CloudTrail anomalies, public exposure findings, impossible travel, unusual role assumption.

Mitigation: Least privilege IAM, CSPM, network controls, encryption, logging, private endpoints, guardrails/IaC scanning.

Supply-chain vulnerabilities

vulnerability

What it is: Weaknesses in third-party software, packages, vendors, CI/CD pipelines, hardware providers, or MSP access become your risk.

Example: Compromised dependency added to a build pipeline steals environment secrets.

Indicators: Unexpected package changes, new maintainer accounts, unusual build outputs, dependency confusion alerts.

Mitigation: SBOM, dependency pinning, package signing, vendor due diligence, CI/CD secret scanning, least-privileged tokens.

Cryptographic vulnerabilities

vulnerability

What it is: Crypto fails when algorithms are weak, protocols are downgraded, keys are reused, randomness is poor, certificates are mishandled, or hashes collide.

Example: Old TLS configuration allows a downgrade to weak ciphers.

Indicators: Deprecated ciphers, certificate warnings, weak hash use, failed audits.

Mitigation: Use modern TLS, strong algorithms, key rotation, certificate management, disable legacy protocols.

Misconfiguration vulnerabilities

vulnerability

What it is: Secure technology becomes insecure when configured poorly: public buckets, permissive firewall rules, default accounts, debug mode, exposed admin panels.

Example: A firewall rule allows 0.0.0.0/0 to a database port.

Indicators: External scan finds admin services; cloud posture tool flags public data; logs show internet probes.

Mitigation: Configuration baselines, drift detection, secure templates, peer review, least privilege, IaC scanning.

Mobile device vulnerabilities

vulnerability

What it is: Mobile risk includes sideloading, jailbreaking/rooting, malicious apps, risky permissions, OS fragmentation, and lost devices.

Example: User sideloads a fake authenticator app that captures OTP codes.

Indicators: MDM compliance failure, unknown app installs, device integrity failure, suspicious SMS permissions.

Mitigation: MDM/MAM, app allow lists, block jailbroken devices, OS updates, remote wipe, user training.

Zero-day vulnerabilities

vulnerability

What it is: A flaw exploited before a patch or public fix is available. Zero-day does not always mean unstoppable; monitoring and layered controls still matter.

Example: An attacker uses an unknown browser vulnerability against executives before vendor patch release.

Indicators: Unexpected exploitation pattern, vendor advisory, EDR behavior alert, crash telemetry.

Mitigation: Defense in depth, least privilege, segmentation, exploit mitigation, rapid containment, threat intel, virtual patching.

Important: “Vulnerability” is not always a CVE. A public storage bucket, default admin password, or over-permissive IAM role may be just as dangerous even if there is no vendor CVE.

For web-app risks, OWASP is a strong reference for categories such as injection, security misconfiguration, vulnerable/outdated components, and logging failures source.

2.4 — Malware indicators

Malware questions usually ask you to identify the type of malware from behavior: encryption, stealth, keystroke capture, self-spreading, disguised software, or condition-based execution.

Ransomware

malware

Meaning: Malware or attacker workflow that encrypts systems and/or steals data to extort payment.

Possible indicators: Mass file renames, ransom notes, disabled backups, unusual SMB traffic, shadow copy deletion.

Mitigation focus: Offline/immutable backups, EDR, segmentation, least privilege, email filtering, incident playbooks.

Trojan

malware

Meaning: Malware disguised as legitimate software or embedded in a trusted-looking file.

Possible indicators: New unknown app, suspicious persistence, beaconing to rare domains.

Mitigation focus: Application allow listing, reputation checks, user education, EDR.

Worm

malware

Meaning: Self-propagating malware that spreads without direct user action once inside reach.

Possible indicators: Rapid lateral movement, many hosts scanning same port, repeated exploitation attempts.

Mitigation focus: Patch fast, segment, block east-west traffic, IPS/IDS, disable vulnerable services.

Spyware

malware

Meaning: Software that monitors behavior, collects data, or exfiltrates information without consent.

Possible indicators: Browser data access, screen capture, keylogging indicators, outbound connections.

Mitigation focus: EDR, permission controls, anti-malware, block unauthorized software.

Bloatware

malware

Meaning: Unnecessary preinstalled or bundled software that increases attack surface and performance load.

Possible indicators: Unexpected startup apps, excessive resource use, vulnerable bundled components.

Mitigation focus: Remove unnecessary software, golden images, asset management.

Virus

malware

Meaning: Malware that attaches to files/programs and spreads when executed.

Possible indicators: File integrity changes, infected executables, AV detections.

Mitigation focus: Anti-malware, backups, least privilege, block unsafe downloads.

Keylogger

malware

Meaning: Captures keystrokes or clipboard data to steal passwords and sensitive information.

Possible indicators: Suspicious keyboard hooks, credential theft, unknown processes reading browser data.

Mitigation focus: Password managers, MFA, EDR, secure input controls, least privilege.

Logic bomb

malware

Meaning: Malicious code triggered by a condition such as a date, event, or account removal.

Possible indicators: Dormant scheduled tasks, suspicious scripts triggered by business dates.

Mitigation focus: Code review, change control, least privilege, monitoring scheduled tasks.

Rootkit

malware

Meaning: Malware designed to hide at OS, bootloader, firmware, or kernel level.

Possible indicators: Security tools disabled, hidden files/processes, boot anomalies.

Mitigation focus: Secure boot, EDR, offline scanning, rebuild from trusted media.

2.4 — Physical, network, application, cryptographic, and password attacks

The exam often gives a scenario and asks you to classify the attack or pick the best mitigation. Read the evidence first, then label the behavior.

Physical attacks

attackindicator

How to understand it: Attackers may physically access systems, clone RFID badges, brute-force locks, tamper with devices, or exploit environmental weaknesses.

Indicators: Tailgating, stolen laptops, cloned access cards, unlocked network closets.

Mitigation focus: Badges, guards, CCTV, access logs, locks, cable control, environmental monitoring.

Denial of Service / DDoS

attackindicator

How to understand it: The goal is availability disruption. Amplified/reflected DDoS abuses third-party systems to multiply traffic toward a victim.

Indicators: Service unreachable, bandwidth saturated, many sources, DNS/NTP reflection traffic.

Mitigation focus: DDoS provider, rate limiting, autoscaling, CDN, Anycast, runbooks, upstream coordination.

DNS attacks

attackindicator

How to understand it: DNS can be spoofed, poisoned, hijacked, tunneled through, or abused with malicious domains.

Indicators: Users redirected, suspicious DNS TXT queries, rare domains, high NXDOMAIN rate.

Mitigation focus: DNSSEC where appropriate, DNS filtering, registrar locks, monitoring, secure resolver config.

Wireless attacks

attackindicator

How to understand it: Wireless adds proximity risk: rogue APs, evil twins, deauthentication, weak encryption, Bluetooth abuse, RF jamming.

Indicators: Duplicate SSIDs, clients connecting to rogue AP, repeated deauth frames.

Mitigation focus: WPA3/802.1X, certificate validation, WIDS/WIPS, site surveys, disable unused radios.

On-path attack

attackindicator

How to understand it: Attacker intercepts or modifies traffic between parties by ARP spoofing, rogue Wi-Fi, proxy abuse, or routing manipulation.

Indicators: Certificate warnings, ARP table changes, unusual gateway MAC, session anomalies.

Mitigation focus: TLS, HSTS, VPN on untrusted networks, switch protections, certificate pinning where useful.

Replay / credential replay

attackindicator

How to understand it: Captured authentication material is reused, especially hashes, tokens, cookies, or old session messages.

Indicators: Same token from new location, pass-the-hash behavior, impossible travel.

Mitigation focus: Nonces/timestamps, short token lifetimes, MFA, channel binding, Kerberos hardening, disable NTLM where possible.

Malicious code

attackindicator

How to understand it: Custom or repurposed code exploits a vulnerability, drops malware, or changes business logic.

Indicators: Unusual scripts, untrusted binaries, new persistence, unexpected outbound callbacks.

Mitigation focus: Code signing, application control, EDR, secure SDLC, least privilege.

Application attacks

attackindicator

How to understand it: Injection, directory traversal, forgery, privilege escalation, buffer overflow, and replay attacks target app logic and input handling.

Indicators: Unexpected admin actions, web errors, strange URL paths, WAF alerts, unauthorized data access.

Mitigation focus: Secure coding, SAST/DAST, WAF, access control testing, logging, dependency patching.

Cryptographic attacks

attackindicator

How to understand it: Downgrade, hash collision, birthday, weak randomness, or poor key handling breaks trust assumptions.

Indicators: Legacy cipher negotiation, duplicate hashes where unique expected, certificate anomalies.

Mitigation focus: Modern protocols, disable weak ciphers, rotate keys, use salted strong hashes, manage certificates.

Password attacks

attackindicator

How to understand it: Password spraying tests common passwords across many accounts; brute force tests many guesses against one or a few accounts.

Indicators: Many users get one or two failures each; account lockouts; login attempts from hosting/VPN nodes.

Mitigation focus: MFA, rate limiting, lockout strategy, banned-password lists, password managers, conditional access.

2.4 — Indicators of malicious activity

An indicator is not proof by itself. It is a clue that must be validated with logs, timelines, identity context, asset context, and known-good baselines. NIST incident response guidance emphasizes preparation, detection, response, recovery, and continuous improvement across cybersecurity risk management source.

Account lockout

indicator

Many failed logins may indicate brute force, password spraying, user error, or a broken service account. Context matters: one account repeatedly failing is different from hundreds of accounts failing once.

Concurrent session usage

indicator

Same user active from two distant devices or protocols may indicate stolen token, shared credentials, or session hijacking.

Blocked content

indicator

Repeated blocks can show a control doing its job, but repeated attempts from one host may show infection or user targeting.

Impossible travel

indicator

A user appears to log in from locations that cannot be physically traveled between in the time window. Cloud proxy/VPN exceptions must be considered.

Resource consumption

indicator

CPU, memory, disk, or network spikes can indicate crypto-mining, malware encryption, data staging, runaway processes, or DDoS.

Resource inaccessibility

indicator

Users cannot access files, apps, DNS, or identity services. This may indicate ransomware, DDoS, misconfiguration, or dependency failure.

Out-of-cycle logging

indicator

Unexpected logging at unusual times can reveal suspicious admin activity, scheduled tasks, or attackers testing persistence.

Published/documented exposure

indicator

Credentials, data, or vulnerabilities appear on paste sites, forums, advisories, or threat-intelligence feeds.

Missing logs

indicator

Gaps in logs are a major indicator: attackers may disable auditing, clear logs, bypass collection, or attack the logging pipeline.

Analyst question to ask every time: “Is this impossible, unusual, unauthorized, or merely unexplained?” Good analysts separate anomalies from confirmed incidents.

2.5 — Mitigation techniques

Mitigations reduce likelihood, impact, or detection/response time. The best answer in a scenario is usually the control that breaks the attacker’s specific path with the least unnecessary business damage.

Segmentation

mitigation

Purpose: Divide the environment into zones so compromise of one area does not mean compromise of everything.

Example: User VLAN cannot directly reach database subnet; production and development are separated; OT and IT are separated.

Why it works: Ransomware and worms lose speed; privilege escalation paths become harder.

Access control: ACLs and permissions

mitigation

Purpose: Define who or what can access a resource and what actions are allowed.

Example: Finance share is limited to finance group; service account can read only one database.

Why it works: Excessive permissions and lateral movement are reduced.

Application allow list

mitigation

Purpose: Only approved applications, scripts, or binaries can run.

Example: Workstations run signed business apps only; unknown EXE from downloads is blocked.

Why it works: Blocks many malware and living-off-the-land chains when configured carefully.

Isolation

mitigation

Purpose: Separate risky systems, users, browsers, files, or workloads from sensitive assets.

Example: Browser isolation for unknown websites; quarantine infected endpoint; sandbox suspicious attachment.

Why it works: Contains damage and lets analysts inspect safely.

Patching

mitigation

Purpose: Apply vendor updates to remove known vulnerabilities. Prioritize by exploitation, exposure, criticality, and business context, not CVSS alone.

Example: Patch internet-facing VPN named in KEV before a low-risk internal desktop bug.

Why it works: Removes known paths used by opportunistic and advanced attackers.

Encryption

mitigation

Purpose: Protect data confidentiality and integrity in transit and at rest; manage keys carefully.

Example: TLS for web apps; full-disk encryption on laptops; encrypted backups.

Why it works: Reduces impact of lost devices, interception, and data theft.

Monitoring

mitigation

Purpose: Collect, correlate, and alert on meaningful events across identity, endpoint, network, cloud, and applications.

Example: SIEM alerts on impossible travel plus mailbox rule creation plus mass download.

Why it works: Turns attacks into detectable incidents before business impact grows.

Least privilege

mitigation

Purpose: Grant only the access required, only for as long as required.

Example: Admin rights are separate from daily accounts; JIT access expires automatically.

Why it works: Limits blast radius and reduces value of stolen accounts.

Configuration enforcement

mitigation

Purpose: Continuously enforce approved baselines and detect drift.

Example: Cloud policy prevents public storage buckets; GPO blocks insecure protocols.

Why it works: Prevents repeated misconfigurations and reduces manual errors.

Decommissioning

mitigation

Purpose: Remove, sanitize, or retire assets that no longer have a business purpose or support path.

Example: Old public test server is shut down and DNS removed; disks are sanitized.

Why it works: Eliminates forgotten attack surface.

Hardening techniques

mitigation

Purpose: Reduce attack surface by secure configuration: endpoint protection, host firewall, HIPS, disabling ports/protocols, changing default passwords, removing unnecessary software.

Example: Disable SMBv1, remove local admin reuse, block inbound workstation connections.

Why it works: Makes exploitation harder and reduces noise in monitoring.

CIS Controls are a useful way to organize practical safeguards such as inventory, secure configuration, access control, malware defenses, logging, and vulnerability management source.

Hardening map: attack → best controls

Hardening is the process of making a system harder to exploit by reducing unnecessary functionality and enforcing secure defaults.

ProblemHardening actionWhy it helps
Open ports / exposed servicesDisable unused services, host firewall, network ACLsRemoves reachable attack paths.
Default passwordsChange default credentials, enforce unique secretsStops automated compromise and botnet enrollment.
Unnecessary softwareRemove bloatware and unused packagesReduces vulnerable components and patch burden.
Malware executionEndpoint protection, EDR, application allow listBlocks or detects malicious behavior.
Weak local network trustSegmentation, host firewall, least privilegeLimits lateral movement after initial compromise.
Configuration driftConfiguration enforcement, baseline scanning, IaC reviewPrevents systems from silently becoming insecure.
Data exposureEncryption, access control, DLP, loggingProtects confidentiality and creates evidence.
Legacy/EOL systemsDecommission, isolate, virtual patch, replacement planControls risk where normal patching is impossible.
Practical priority order: know assets → remove internet exposure → fix exploited vulnerabilities → enforce MFA → least privilege → backups → monitor → test response.

Real-world case studies mapped to Domain 2

These cases help convert abstract exam terms into real cyber incidents. For Security+, you do not need every technical detail; you need to map actor, vector, vulnerability, indicator, and mitigation.

SolarWinds Orion supply-chain compromise

case study

Threat actor: nation-state/APT. Vector: trusted software update. Vulnerability class: malicious update / supply chain. Indicator: unusual network callbacks, identity abuse, persistence. Mitigation lesson: do not blindly trust signed updates; monitor vendor software behavior, restrict vendor products, and maintain incident response capability.

Reference: source

MOVEit Transfer exploitation by CL0P

case study

Threat actor: organized crime/extortion. Vector: internet-facing managed file transfer app. Vulnerability class: zero-day SQL injection. Indicator: web shell/malware, data theft, unusual file access. Mitigation lesson: internet-facing apps need asset inventory, emergency patching, logging, and breach notification workflow.

Reference: source

Apache Log4j / Log4Shell

case study

Threat actor: many actors from scanners to criminal groups. Vector: vulnerable software dependency. Vulnerability class: remote code execution in a widely used logging library. Indicator: exploit strings in logs, callbacks to attacker infrastructure, crypto-mining, web shell attempts. Mitigation lesson: know software dependencies, use SBOM/SCA, patch fast, and monitor after patching.

Reference: source

Colonial Pipeline ransomware

case study

Threat actor: ransomware crime group. Vector: credential/access abuse. Vulnerability class: weak identity and remote access control. Indicator: ransomware activity, operational disruption, extortion communication. Mitigation lesson: MFA, tested backups, segmentation, and incident response matter for business continuity.

Reference: source

Equifax data breach

case study

Threat actor: external attackers. Vector: vulnerable web application. Vulnerability class: unpatched Apache Struts and weak vulnerability management. Indicator: abnormal database access/exfiltration. Mitigation lesson: patch SLAs, inventory, certificate/monitoring maintenance, and segmentation prevent known-vulnerability breaches.

Reference: source

Mirai IoT botnet

case study

Threat actor: criminal/opportunistic. Vector: internet-exposed IoT using default credentials. Vulnerability class: default credentials and poor device hardening. Indicator: device scanning and DDoS traffic. Mitigation lesson: change defaults, restrict admin access, patch firmware, and segment IoT.

Reference: source

Scattered Spider social engineering

case study

Threat actor: organized cybercrime with strong social engineering. Vector: help desk, SIM swap, push bombing, impersonation. Vulnerability class: process weakness around identity proofing. Indicator: MFA fatigue, new device enrollment, help-desk reset anomalies. Mitigation lesson: verification workflows, phishing-resistant MFA, and support-team training are technical controls plus process controls.

Reference: source

Common exam traps

These are the distinctions that usually decide close multiple-choice questions.

TrapCorrect way to think
Threat actor vs motivationActor is who; motivation is why. Example: organized crime is the actor; financial gain is the motivation.
Threat vector vs vulnerabilityVector is the path used to reach the target; vulnerability is the weakness exploited. Email is a vector; weak macro policy is a vulnerability.
Indicator vs attackIndicator is evidence after or during activity. Account lockout is an indicator; password spraying is an attack.
CVSS vs riskCVSS measures technical severity. Real risk also includes exploit activity, asset exposure, business impact, and compensating controls.
Zero-day vs unpatched known vulnerabilityZero-day means no available fix at the time of exploitation. A five-year-old unpatched CVE is not a zero-day.
DDoS amplified vs reflectedReflected means traffic bounces off third parties; amplified means small request produces larger response. Many attacks are both.
On-path vs replayOn-path intercepts active traffic. Replay reuses captured authentication or messages later.
Misconfiguration vs vulnerabilityA misconfiguration is often self-created exposure, not necessarily a vendor bug.

Quick practice questions

Click an answer to reveal the explanation. These are original practice questions for learning, not exam dumps.

Q1. An attacker sends one common password attempt to hundreds of accounts. What is this?

Explanation: Password spraying spreads a common guess across many accounts to avoid lockouts.

Q2. A user logs in from Toronto and five minutes later from Singapore. Which indicator fits best?

Explanation: Impossible travel indicates two locations are not physically plausible in the time window.

Q3. A vendor's signed update installs a backdoor. What category is strongest?

Explanation: The trust relationship and update channel were abused.

Q4. Which mitigation most directly limits ransomware spreading between departments?

Explanation: Segmentation reduces lateral movement and blast radius.

Q5. A web app concatenates user input into SQL queries. Which mitigation is best?

Explanation: Parameterized queries separate code from data.

Q6. An attacker intercepts traffic on a rogue Wi-Fi network. What attack type is this?

Explanation: On-path attacks intercept traffic between parties.

Q7. A flaw is exploited before the vendor releases a patch. What is it called?

Explanation: Zero-day refers to exploitation before a fix is available.

Q8. A help desk is tricked into resetting MFA for an attacker. What vector dominates?

Explanation: The weak point is the human/process interaction.

Q9. What is the main difference between CVSS and business risk?

Explanation: Risk requires exposure, exploitation, impact, and controls.

Q10. A mobile user installs an app outside the official store. Which topic fits?

Explanation: Sideloading bypasses official app-store controls.

Q11. Which is a typical rootkit goal?

Explanation: Rootkits are designed to hide at deep system levels.

Q12. A public storage bucket exposes customer backups. What vulnerability type is most likely?

Explanation: The cloud control was configured insecurely.

Q13. A guest VM breaks isolation and affects the host. What is this?

Explanation: VM escape crosses the guest-to-host boundary.

Q14. Repeated DNS TXT queries to rare domains could indicate what?

Explanation: DNS is often abused for command-and-control or exfiltration.

Q15. Which actor is most associated with espionage and long-term stealth?

Explanation: Nation-states commonly pursue espionage with high resources.

Q16. Which mitigation removes forgotten attack surface?

Explanation: Retiring old assets eliminates unused exposure.

Q17. A fake domain mimics a brand with a misspelling. What is this?

Explanation: Typosquatting uses confusingly similar domains.

Q18. A small DNS request causes a much larger response to the victim. What describes it?

Explanation: Amplification multiplies traffic volume.

Q19. What control best prevents unnecessary admin rights from increasing blast radius?

Explanation: Least privilege limits what a compromised account can do.

Q20. Which indicator is suspicious because attackers may try to hide traces?

Explanation: Log gaps can indicate tampering or disabled collection.
